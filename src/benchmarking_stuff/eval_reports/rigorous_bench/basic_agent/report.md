# Comparative Analysis: Obergefell v. Hodges and Schalk and Kopf v. Austria - Two Courts' Approaches to Same-Sex Marriage

## Introduction

The legal recognition of same-sex marriage represents one of the most significant civil rights developments of the 21st century. Two landmark cases—**Obergefell v. Hodges** (2015) in the United States Supreme Court and **Schalk and Kopf v. Austria** (2010) in the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR)—illustrate fundamentally different judicial approaches to this issue. While both cases addressed the rights of same-sex couples to marry, they reached dramatically different conclusions and employed distinct legal reasoning. This report examines these two landmark decisions, comparing how the U.S. Supreme Court and the European Court of Human Rights approached the question of marriage equality, analyzing their legal frameworks, reasoning, and impacts on same-sex couples' rights.

## Case Overviews

### Obergefell v. Hodges (2015)

**Background and Facts:**
Obergefell v. Hodges was a consolidation of six lower-court cases from Michigan, Ohio, Kentucky, and Tennessee involving 14 same-sex couples and two men whose same-sex partners had died [1]. The lead plaintiff, James Obergefell, sought to have his Maryland marriage to John Arthur recognized on Arthur's Ohio death certificate after Arthur's death from ALS [2]. The central legal question was whether the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to license marriages between two people of the same sex and to recognize such marriages performed in other states [3].

**Legal Framework:**
The case was decided under the **Fourteenth Amendment** to the U.S. Constitution, specifically the **Due Process Clause** and **Equal Protection Clause** [4]. The petitioners argued that state bans on same-sex marriage violated both clauses by denying fundamental rights and discriminating based on sexual orientation.

**Decision and Reasoning:**
In a 5-4 decision authored by Justice Anthony Kennedy, the Supreme Court held that:
1. The right to marry is a fundamental right inherent in individual liberty
2. This right extends to same-sex couples under both Due Process and Equal Protection principles
3. States must license marriages between same-sex couples
4. States must recognize same-sex marriages performed in other states [5]

Justice Kennedy's majority opinion emphasized four principles:
- The right to personal choice regarding marriage is inherent in individual autonomy
- Marriage supports a two-person union unlike any other in its importance to committed individuals
- Marriage safeguards children and families
- Marriage is a keystone of social order [6]

The Court found that excluding same-sex couples from marriage "demeans gays and lesbians" and "harm[s] and humiliate[s] the children of same-sex couples" [7]. The decision explicitly stated that "the right to marry is a fundamental right inherent in the liberty of the person, and under the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment couples of the same sex may not be deprived of that right and that liberty" [8].

### Schalk and Kopf v. Austria (2010)

**Background and Facts:**
Horst Schalk and Johann Kopf, an Austrian same-sex couple in a stable relationship since the mid-1980s, sought to marry in Vienna in 2003 but were refused because Austrian law defined marriage as a union between a man and a woman [9]. They challenged this refusal before the European Court of Human Rights, arguing violations of the European Convention on Human Rights.

**Legal Framework:**
The case was decided under the **European Convention on Human Rights**, specifically:
- **Article 12**: Right to marry ("Men and women of marriageable age have the right to marry and to found a family")
- **Article 8**: Right to respect for private and family life
- **Article 14**: Prohibition of discrimination [10]

**Decision and Reasoning:**
In a unanimous decision, the ECtHR held that:
1. Article 12 does not impose an obligation on states to grant same-sex couples access to marriage
2. There was no violation of Article 8 taken alone or in conjunction with Article 14
3. The differential treatment was within Austria's "margin of appreciation" [11]

The Court's reasoning included several key points:
- The wording of Article 12 ("men and women") suggested it was intended to apply only to opposite-sex couples
- The Convention should be interpreted as a "living instrument" but the Court found no European consensus on same-sex marriage in 2010
- Austria had introduced registered partnerships in 2010, providing legal recognition for same-sex couples
- The Court acknowledged that same-sex couples could enjoy "family life" under Article 8 but found no obligation to grant marriage rights [12]

## Comparative Analysis

### 1. Legal Frameworks and Constitutional Interpretation

**U.S. Supreme Court (Obergefell):**
- Used **substantive due process** analysis, finding marriage to be a fundamental right
- Applied **equal protection** principles to prohibit discrimination based on sexual orientation
- Emphasized **individual liberty and autonomy** as core constitutional values
- Viewed the Constitution as an evolving document that must adapt to new understandings of liberty and equality [13]

**European Court of Human Rights (Schalk and Kopf):**
- Applied **textual interpretation** of Article 12, focusing on the phrase "men and women"
- Used the **"living instrument" doctrine** but found insufficient consensus among member states
- Applied the **"margin of appreciation" doctrine**, granting states discretion in social policy
- Separated analysis of marriage (Article 12) from family life protections (Article 8) [14]

### 2. Judicial Philosophy and Role

**U.S. Approach:**
- **Active judicial role** in protecting minority rights against majority will
- Willingness to **overturn state laws** (invalidated marriage laws in more than half the states)
- Emphasis on **individual dignity** and equal citizenship
- Recognition of **evolving social understandings** of marriage and family [15]

**European Approach:**
- **Judicial restraint** and deference to national legislatures
- Emphasis on **subsidiarity** and respect for national traditions
- **Incremental approach** to rights expansion
- Focus on **European consensus** as prerequisite for imposing obligations [16]

### 3. Treatment of Same-Sex Relationships

**Obergefell's Progressive Stance:**
- Recognized same-sex relationships as **equal in dignity and worth** to opposite-sex relationships
- Found that **excluding same-sex couples** from marriage violated constitutional principles
- Emphasized the **harm to children** of same-sex couples from exclusion
- Declared that **love and commitment** are the same regardless of sexual orientation [17]

**Schalk and Kopf's Cautious Approach:**
- Acknowledged same-sex couples could constitute "family life" under Article 8
- Found no **obligation to provide marriage access** under the Convention
- Accepted **registered partnerships** as sufficient legal recognition
- Left the question of marriage to **national democratic processes** [18]

### 4. Impact and Aftermath

**Obergefell's Transformative Impact:**
- Immediately legalized same-sex marriage in all 50 U.S. states
- Affected an estimated **500,000 same-sex couples** in the U.S.
- Generated **$1.58 billion in economic impact** from weddings
- Provided federal recognition and benefits to same-sex married couples
- Faced ongoing political and legal challenges but remains binding precedent [19]

**Schalk and Kopf's Limited Impact:**
- Did not require any state to legalize same-sex marriage
- Allowed continued **national variation** across Europe
- Recognized that **some form of legal recognition** was required under Article 8
- Paved the way for subsequent cases requiring partnership recognition
- Reflected the **diverse legal landscape** in Europe regarding same-sex unions [20]

### 5. Subsequent Developments

**After Obergefell:**
- The decision faced **multiple legislative challenges** but was reaffirmed
- **Masterpiece Cakeshop v. Colorado Civil Rights Commission** (2018) addressed religious objections
- Ongoing debates about **religious liberty exemptions**
- Continued expansion of LGBTQ+ rights in other areas [21]

**After Schalk and Kopf:**
- **Oliari and Others v. Italy** (2015) required Italy to provide some form of legal recognition
- **Fedotova and Others v. Russia** (2021) found Russia violated Article 8 by failing to provide any legal framework
- **Strasbourg jurisprudence** has evolved but still stops short of requiring marriage equality
- Growing **European consensus** with 21 of 46 Council of Europe members allowing same-sex marriage [22]

## Key Differences in Judicial Approaches

### 1. Constitutional vs. International Law Framework
The U.S. Supreme Court operated within a **national constitutional framework** with binding authority over all states, while the ECtHR functions within an **international human rights system** that respects national sovereignty through the margin of appreciation doctrine.

### 2. Fundamental Rights vs. Progressive Realization
Obergefell treated marriage as a **fundamental right** requiring immediate and full protection, while Schalk and Kopf viewed same-sex marriage rights as subject to **progressive realization** based on evolving consensus.

### 3. Judicial Activism vs. Subsidiarity
The U.S. Court demonstrated **judicial activism** in overturning numerous state laws, while the European Court emphasized **subsidiarity**, deferring to national democratic processes.

### 4. Individual Dignity vs. State Discretion
Obergefell centered on **individual dignity and equality**, while Schalk and Kopf balanced individual rights against **state discretion** in social policy.

## Conclusion

Obergefell v. Hodges and Schalk and Kopf v. Austria represent two fundamentally different judicial approaches to same-sex marriage rights. The U.S. Supreme Court took a **transformative, rights-based approach** that immediately established marriage equality nationwide based on constitutional principles of liberty and equality. In contrast, the European Court of Human Rights adopted a **cautious, incremental approach** that respected national diversity while acknowledging evolving understandings of family life.

These differences reflect deeper distinctions between the **U.S. constitutional system** with its strong judicial review and the **European human rights system** with its emphasis on subsidiarity and margin of appreciation. While Obergefell created a uniform national standard, Schalk and Kopf allowed for continued variation across Europe, reflecting different conceptions of judicial role, federalism, and the pace of social change.

Both decisions, however, contributed to the global expansion of LGBTQ+ rights, with Obergefell providing a model for judicial leadership in rights protection and Schalk and Kopf establishing that same-sex couples are entitled to some form of legal recognition under international human rights law. The contrasting approaches illustrate how different legal systems balance individual rights against democratic processes and social traditions in addressing one of the most significant civil rights issues of our time.
